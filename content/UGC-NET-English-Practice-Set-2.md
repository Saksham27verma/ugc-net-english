**Read the following passage and answer the questions that follow.**

> Every technology arrives with a story about itself, and the story is usually about liberation. The machine will free us from labour; the network will free us from distance; the algorithm will free us from the tedium of judgement. Such promises are not lies, but they are partial in a particular way: they describe what the technology adds and are silent about what it quietly withdraws. The motor car did free the traveller from the timetable, and it also removed from the street the possibility of a child playing in it. Neither the gain nor the loss can be read off from the device itself; both are the work of the arrangements we build around it, and those arrangements are political. To ask of a new instrument only whether it works is therefore to ask the smaller half of the question. The larger half is who is now able to do what, and at whose expense; and this half is rarely settled by engineers, though it is often decided by them in effect, simply because nobody else was in the room.

## Comprehension — Prose (Q.146–Q.150)

**Read the following passage carefully and answer the questions that follow.**

> The realist novel achieved its authority by a trick that has since become invisible: it persuaded readers that a narrative voice could stand nowhere in particular. The omniscient narrator of the nineteenth century is not a person but a vantage — above the town, inside every skull, unhurried, unimplicated, apparently without interests of its own. The trick worked because the vantage flattered the reader, who was invited to share it. Modernist fiction did not so much destroy this convention as expose its cost. Once the narration is lodged inside a single consciousness, everything gains the vividness of partiality and loses the authority of survey; the town can no longer be described, only glimpsed from a window.
